<h2>What Idaho lodging tax dollars can legally fund</h2>
<p class="lede2">Idaho has no general lodging tax authority for cities or counties. Local room taxes exist in exactly two forms: the resort city local option tax under Idaho Code 50-1044 to 50-1049, open only to cities of 10,000 or fewer people that pass a 60 percent vote, and the auditorium district room tax under Idaho Code 67-4901 to 67-4930, a special district that may charge up to 5 percent to build and run public venues. The state's own 2 percent Travel and Convention tax under Idaho Code 67-4718 funds the Idaho Travel Council grant program, which is the only statewide competitive pot and the only one with written eligibility rules. There is no Idaho equivalent of Washington's RCW 67.28 use list or Oregon's 70/30 split: a resort city spends the money on the purposes its voters approved on the ballot, and an auditorium district spends it on the facilities named in its petition. Nothing changed in these statutes in the 2025 or 2026 sessions.</p>
<div class="cols">
<div>
<h3>Resort city taxes, Idaho Code 50-1044 to 50-1049</h3>
<ul>
<li>Who qualifies: a "resort city with a population not in excess of ten thousand (10,000)" that "derives the major portion of its economic well-being from businesses catering to the recreational needs and meeting the needs of people traveling to that destination city for an extended period of time" (50-1044). The cap is why Coeur d'Alene, Boise, Idaho Falls and every other city above 10,000 has no local lodging tax of its own. The section has not been amended since 2013.</li>
<li>Voter approval: "A sixty per cent (60%) majority of the voters of any resort city voting on the question" must approve (50-1046). A rejected or approved tax cannot return to the ballot for eleven months (50-1044).</li>
<li>Three taxes are allowed under 50-1046: "(a) an occupancy tax upon hotel, motel, and other sleeping accommodations rented or leased for a period of thirty (30) days or less; (b) a tax upon liquor by-the-drink, wine and beer sold at retail for consumption on the licensed premises; and (c) a sales tax upon part or all of sales subject to taxation under chapter 36, title 63, Idaho Code." There is no statutory rate cap. Sandpoint voters took their lodging tax from 7 to 14 percent in 2022; McCall charges 7 percent on lodging plus a 1 percent general sales tax.</li>
<li>Uses: the statute does not list them. Collections become "city revenue for any lawful corporate purpose" that the voters approved (50-1047). The ballot ordinance must "state and define the specific tax to be approved," state the exact rate, state "the exact purpose or purposes for which the revenues derived from the tax will be used," and state the duration. None of those four can be changed without going back to the voters.</li>
<li>Property tax relief: a city may create a "city property tax relief fund" and must put any collections above the budgeted amount into it, to offset city property taxes the following year (50-1045). Many ballot questions list this fund as one of the purposes.</li>
<li>Collection: the city may contract with the State Tax Commission or administer the tax itself (50-1049). The Commission's list shows 23 cities with a local option tax: Bellevue, Bonners Ferry, Cascade, Crouch, Donnelly, Driggs, Hailey, Harrison, Irwin, Kellogg, Ketchum, Lava Hot Springs, Mackay, McCall, Ponderay, Riggins, Salmon, Sandpoint, Stanley, Sun Valley, Swan Valley, Tetonia and Victor.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div>
<h3>Auditorium districts, Idaho Code 67-4901 to 67-4930</h3>
<ul>
<li>What they are for: a district may build, operate, maintain, market and manage "public auditoriums, exhibition halls, convention centers, sports arenas and facilities of a similar nature" (67-4902). The chapter never uses the word tourism. The board may "promote any functions for said district" (67-4912(m)) but is told not "to compete with existing facilities and services in the district." Promoting the district's own venues and the conventions that fill them is inside the power; a grant program for outside events is not something the statute describes.</li>
<li>Formation: a petition signed by at least 10 percent of qualified electors, stating the facilities, the budget, the boundaries and "the maximum tax rate that the board will be authorized to levy or impose" (67-4904), a district court hearing, and an election carried by a simple majority (67-4907). Districts formed after July 1, 2001 must be one contiguous area made up of all or part of one or more cities or counties, which makes this the only Idaho room tax that can cross a county line.</li>
<li>The room tax: the board may levy "a hotel/motel room sales tax not to exceed five percent (5%) of the receipts derived by hotels and motels" (67-4917B), by resolution, no separate vote, with stays over 30 days and the Idaho Ronald McDonald House exempt. For districts formed after 2001 the rate "shall not exceed the maximum tax rate authorized in the petition." The State Tax Commission collects it and remits monthly (67-4917C). Districts of 25,000 or more people cannot levy property tax (67-4907, 67-4913), so the room tax is the whole budget. The Idaho Supreme Court upheld the tax against a hotel challenge in Ameritel Inns v. Greater Boise Auditorium District, 141 Idaho 849 (2005).</li>
<li>Governance: five elected directors, six-year staggered terms, three for a quorum (67-4907 to 67-4911). Debt of $75,000 or more needs a district election (67-4922).</li>
<li>The districts: Greater Boise (5 percent, $11.4 million in 2024, owns Boise Centre), Idaho Falls (Mountain America Center), Pocatello-Chubbuck (Mountain View Event Center), Nampa (approved November 5, 2024 with about 62 percent, for the Ford Idaho Center and Nampa Civic Center) and the Mountain Community Center District in the McCall-Donnelly area (approved November 4, 2025 with about 64 percent, 5 percent, roughly $1.5 million a year for a public fieldhouse). The Tax Commission began administering the two newest in spring 2026.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div>
<h3>The state 2 percent and the Idaho Travel Council</h3>
<ul>
<li>The tax: Idaho Code 67-4718 levies a 2 percent assessment on each "sale," defined in 67-4711 as renting a place to sleep "for a period of less than thirty-one (31) continuous days" through a hotel, motel or private campground; hotel/motel includes condominiums, townhouses and any other establishment that makes a sale, which is how vacation rentals are caught. The Tax Commission collects it alongside the 6 percent sales tax and the balance goes to the Idaho travel and convention account at the Department of Commerce.</li>
<li>The split: 67-4717 sends half, after administration, to a regional grant program and half to statewide promotion. Commerce's guidelines state it as 10 percent administration, 45 percent statewide marketing (Visit Idaho) and 45 percent "for grants within the region where the tax was collected." Each of the seven planning regions in 67-4711 gets back its own share, and unspent regional money "shall not lapse."</li>
<li>Who can apply: "local nonprofit organizations having as their primary purpose the promotion of travel and conventions" (67-4717). Commerce's 2026 guidelines say nonprofit, incorporated organizations with a viable travel or convention program, with preference to destination marketing organizations focused on overnight visitation. Cities, counties, for-profit producers and universities are not eligible. HB 861 (2026, Rep. Jason Monks) would have added colleges and universities; the House Revenue and Taxation Committee killed it in March 2026 after the Idaho Outfitters and Guides Association and rural legislators objected to tourism money backfilling the general fund.</li>
<li>What it funds: advertising in all media, familiarization tours, "sponsorship of major events and other partnerships," trade shows, public relations, research, training and capital outlay, all aimed at overnight visitors from beyond a two-hour drive. Entertainment, alcohol, honoraria, grant writing and membership fees are out. A 12.5 percent cash match is required, documented by an actual exchange of money.</li>
<li>The cycle: applications open in early February and close at the end of March (March 30, 2026 at 4:30 p.m. Mountain for the 2026 round), applicants present to the Council in Boise in early May, final budgets are due at the end of May, awards come at the end of July once the fiscal year's collections are known, and the grant period runs August 1 through September 30 of the following year. On July 31, 2026 the Council awarded $11,155,592 to 24 grantees, its largest round, including $1,847,022 to Visit Boise, $1,330,000 to the Coeur d'Alene Convention and Visitors Bureau, $2,064,539 to Yellowstone Teton Territory and $495,000 to the Sun Valley Marketing Alliance. The 2027 round should open in February 2027.</li>
<li>The Council: eight members appointed by the governor to three-year terms (67-4712), advisory to the Department of Commerce, which runs the program.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div>
<h3>How decisions are made</h3>
<ul>
<li>Resort cities: by the ballot ordinance first and the council second. The voters fix the purposes and the council spends within them through the annual budget. Where the ballot listed broad categories (streets, parks, public safety, housing, "visitor services and promotion," property tax relief) the council has wide discretion; where it named a split, the split is binding. McCall holds 3 percent of its lodging tax in trust and awards it each year to applicants for projects that benefit "quality of life and/or tourism." Hailey directs its added 1 percent on rooms to housing and air service. Sandpoint's 2022 measure sends half to streets, pathways and property tax relief through 2035. Victor and Driggs each asked voters in November 2025 to go from 6 to 8 percent on lodging with long purpose lists, and Ketchum is drafting a 2 percent lodging increase for streets for November 2026.</li>
<li>No committee is required. Idaho has nothing like Washington's lodging tax advisory committee. A few cities create one by ordinance (McCall's LOT commission reviews applications); most decide in the budget hearing.</li>
<li>Auditorium districts: by the elected five-member board, within the facilities named in the petition and the powers in 67-4912. Boards fund their own venues, debt service and marketing, and may sponsor events that bring conventions or room nights to those venues. They answer to district voters, not to a city council.</li>
<li>The statewide money is decided by the Idaho Travel Council in May and July, on staff scoring and in-person presentations, and flows to the regional DMO or chamber that applied. Most regional grantees run co-op advertising, trade show and event sponsorship programs of their own with that money.</li>
<li>Recent legislation: HJR 5 and H 375 (2025, Rep. Britt Raybould) would have put a constitutional amendment to voters allowing any city or county a local option sales tax of up to 2 percent for four years for a named project, while phasing out auditorium districts. Both were introduced for discussion and went no further. H 388 (2025), a county lodging sales tax for property tax relief, died in House Revenue and Taxation. A 2026 draft to give Boise State a seat on the Greater Boise board and a share of its room tax was never introduced. Idaho Code 50-1044 last changed in 2013, 67-4917B in 2001 and 67-4717 in 1985.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<details>
<summary>What this means for an event producer in Idaho</summary>
<div class="talk">
<p>Start by finding out where the money sits, because in Idaho it is never in one place. In a resort city it sits with the council, and the question is whether the ballot language covers you. Read the ordinance (Hailey's, McCall's and Victor's are online) and look for words like promotion, visitor services, events, economic development or quality of life. If they are there, you are asking for a budget line or a grant, and the only process is the one the city invented. If the ballot says streets, housing and property tax relief and nothing else, the council cannot legally fund you from that tax no matter how many room nights you bring, and the honest ask is for in-kind support from the general fund. In an auditorium district the money sits with a five-member board whose statute is about buildings, so the pitch is a venue booking or a sponsorship tied to its own facility, framed as filling the Boise Centre, Mountain America Center, the Ford Idaho Center or the future McCall fieldhouse, not as a community grant.</p>
<p>The Idaho Travel Council grant is the main statewide route and a for-profit producer cannot hold it. The regional DMO can: Visit Boise, the Coeur d'Alene CVB, Yellowstone Teton Territory, Visit Sun Valley and the other regional grantees apply each March for their region's share and may list "sponsorship of major events" in their budgets. That makes the DMO's planning window, December through March, the time to be in the room with a room-night estimate, a hotel block plan and a marketing budget that the DMO can book as its own eligible cost with a 12.5 percent cash match. Chambers under contract to a resort city for marketing sit in the same position one level down.</p>
<p>Do not expect reporting leverage. Idaho does not require cities to report lodging tax spending to the state, there is no tourism definition to argue from and no statutory split to point to, so the case is local: who benefits, which hotels will say so, and whether the purpose fits the words on the ballot. Bring the ballot language to the meeting.</p>
