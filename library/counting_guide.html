How to count room nights and attendance

Washington law asks two things of every lodging tax grant. The applicant gives estimates before the money is awarded, and the recipient reports the "actual number" of people afterward (RCW 67.28.1816(2)). JLARC compiles those reports but states that it does "not independently verify the information." The committee that reads the report is the only check. In Washington's 2022 to 2024 state data, about 20 percent of event reports list actual room nights identical to the estimate, and about 20 percent come in under half the estimate. This section is for producers who write those reports and the committees that review them.

What the report has to show

  • The statute counts people traveling "(A) Away from their place of residence or business and staying overnight in paid accommodations; (B) To a place fifty miles or more one way from their place of residence or business for the day or staying overnight; or (C) From another country or state." The JLARC form adds total attendance, people who did not pay for lodging, and paid lodging nights, each with a predicted figure, an actual figure and a method.
  • JLARC defines a lodging night as "one or more persons occupying a room for a single night." Room nights are rooms, not people. Six people in two rooms for two nights are four room nights.
  • JLARC accepts "a good faith best estimate" where an actual count is "impractical," but asks recipients to report the method with the figure. Spokane Valley requires the method named in the application to be used again for the final report; Vancouver requires "an explanation of how attendance figures were calculated."
  • Paid lodging nights and overnight visitors are different fields. A guest staying with family is an overnight visitor who did not pay. A visitor who drove 80 miles and went home counts in the 50-mile field and in no lodging field.

Methods, most to least reliable

  • Hotel pickup by night (Direct Count). The hotel or housing bureau's report of rooms booked in the event block, by property and night. Attach the signed pickup report and the block contract. Treat it as a floor: a Tourism Economics study of more than 170 events found 34.1 percent of group room nights were booked outside the block. Destinations International's room demand tool estimates those outside nights from attendee ZIP codes.
  • Tickets, scans and registrations (Direct Count). Platform exports of tickets sold, scanned entries by day, and participant registrations. Attach the summary export. Registration and checkout forms can ask home ZIP code, party size and lodging plans, which turns a head count into a distance and lodging count. Stay-to-play tournaments, where teams must book through designated hotels, give the cleanest room data.
  • Representative Survey. A random sample (every nth person or group at the gate, not volunteers) that can be projected to all attendees, with a stated margin of error. The Audience Agency's sampling guide lists 377 completed surveys for a 5 percent margin at audiences of 20,000 or more and treats 10 percent as the upper limit. Attach the questionnaire, sampling plan, sample size, margin of error and the expansion math.
  • Post-event lodging partner survey (Direct Count). JLARC counts information "collected directly from businesses, such as hotels." Ask each property for rooms attributed to the event and the same weekend last year. Attach the list of properties asked, who answered, and their figures. Attribution is the property's judgment, so say how they made it.
  • Indirect Count. Wristbands, parking counts, raffle tickets, crowd estimates. Attach the source counts and the conversion used (for example, people per car).
  • Structured Estimate. A calculation from known facts, such as event area divided by 3 square feet per person. That measures capacity, not attendance; attach the inputs.
  • Informal Survey. Nonrandom responses. JLARC says results "cannot be projected to the entire visitor population." Report the raw responses, not a projection.
  • Other. Requires a written explanation. Without one, a committee has no basis to accept the figure.

Common mistakes

  • Copying the estimate into the actual column. An actual that matches the forecast to the unit is a sign no count was made.
  • Counting day visitors as overnight. Traveling 50 miles and staying overnight are separate tests; most 50-mile visitors at a one-day event go home.
  • Counting people as room nights. Divide paid overnight visitors by people per room, then multiply by nights stayed.
  • Counting scans as people. A three-day pass scanned each day is one person, three entries.
  • Claiming the same nights under two grants. If a neighboring city or the county also funded the event, report nights by the city where guests stayed and disclose the other grant. JLARC warns that splitting activities "may create opportunities for error such as double reporting."
  • Total attendance times a guessed percentage. "20,000 attendees, 30 percent stayed overnight" with no sample behind the 30 percent is an estimate, not an actual. Name it as such.

What a good post-event report gives

  • Total attendance: unique people, entries if different, and the method.
  • Home location known for how many attendees (ZIP from tickets, registration or survey), and from that the share and count traveling 50 miles or more and from out of state.
  • Overnight visitors: paid and unpaid, with the source.
  • Average people per room and average nights stayed.
  • Paid lodging nights: block pickup by property and night, an estimate of nights booked outside the block with its basis, the total, and the city where they were stayed.
  • Predicted figure beside each actual, with one line on why they differ.
  • Other public funders of the same activity.
  • Attachments: pickup reports, ticketing export summary, survey instrument and results, lodging partner responses.
