Oregon HB 4148: a decision brief for councils

HB 4148 (2026, the LOCAL Act, Oregon Laws 2026 chapter 121) took effect June 5, 2026. On January 1, 2027 the required tourism share of post-2003 local transient lodging tax revenue drops from 70 percent to 50 percent, and the share allowed for city or county services rises from 30 to 50 percent. The law permits the change; it does not require it. Each city and county with a lodging tax now has a choice to make, and from September 2027 it has to report what it chose.

What changes and when

  • The new split is operative January 1, 2027 and applies to net revenue collected "before, on or after" the effective date, including unspent reserves. For a city on a July to June fiscal year, that date falls mid-year.
  • "City or county services" now covers "emergency or nonemergency services," including services a special district provides in place of the city or county.
  • The tourism half gains a new use: "resiliency grants for small businesses in the restaurant and lodging industry." The bill does not define resiliency grant or small business; the council sets the terms.
  • Pre-2003 taxes: the old rule that a city "may not decrease the percentage" spent on tourism is lifted. New subsection (7)(c) allows a decrease, down to the 50 percent floor, "regardless of when the percentage went into effect."
  • Reporting: each taxing city and county files with the Legislative Revenue Officer by September 1 of each odd-numbered year starting 2027, covering the two prior fiscal years: rates, revenue, the amount and percentage spent on tourism promotion (including DMO contracts), facilities, resiliency grants and services, capital reserves, allocation changes and oversight actions. DMOs report to the city for inclusion. LOC or AOC may file for members. The reporting section sunsets January 2, 2041; a Legislative Revenue Officer study of the split is due September 15, 2034.
  • HB 4134 is separate. The state rate rises from 1.5 to 2.75 percent for nights beginning on or after January 1, 2027. The 1.25-point increase is a "nature conservation fee" for the Recovering Oregon's Wildlife Fund, not tourism, so Travel Oregon's grant programs do not grow from it. Guests in most taxing cities will pay a higher combined rate in 2027.

What has happened so far

  • Lane County proposed using lodging tax reserves for rural sheriff patrols and courthouse work. Travel Lane County objected. On June 23, 2026 the board voted 4 to 1 to drop the transfer from the lodging tax reserve and fund one year of patrols ($1,997,919) from general fund reserves.
  • Jefferson County wrote in support of HB 4148 in February 2026, citing tourism-related calls for law enforcement, search and rescue, fire and EMS.
  • Deschutes County held an April 6, 2026 work session on its allocation with no recommended motion, and noted its Visit Central Oregon contract renews automatically and requires written notice before funding is cut.
  • Curry County's budget committee was told in May 2026 the shift could add about $600,000 in unrestricted funds, with part possibly going to a competitive grant program for tourism, facades and marketing.
  • Positions: the League of Oregon Cities called the bill a long-term priority and tells cities to "consider this flexibility when crafting a budget." The Association of Oregon Counties supported it. The Oregon Restaurant and Lodging Association moved from opposition to neutral once the floor was set at 50 percent. Travel Lane County, Visit Bend and several chambers opposed it. This site has not found a council vote pledging to keep 70 percent.

Options and what each means for event producers

  • Keep tourism at 70 percent or the current share. No change to grant pools or DMO contracts. Producers apply as before.
  • Move to 50 percent for services. Frees up to 20 points of post-2003 revenue, and possibly more of pre-2003 revenue, for services. Tourism grant pools and DMO budgets shrink unless revenue growth offsets the cut. The services half has more room for event costs a city already bears, such as police overtime and street closures.
  • Use part of the tourism half for resiliency grants. Keeps money on the tourism side but shifts it from promotion to restaurant and lodging businesses. Less for event marketing unless the pool grows.
  • Put a set share into an open event grant round. Keeps or reassigns tourism money and publishes criteria, a deadline and reporting rules. Gives producers a direct route instead of applying through a DMO.

Questions to answer before deciding

  • What does the freed money replace, and is that cost one-time or ongoing?
  • Which revenue is post-2003, which is pre-2003, and what share of each goes to tourism today?
  • Do DMO or chamber contracts fix the percentage, and what notice do they require?
  • How will the choice appear in the September 2027 report, and who collects DMO data for it?
  • Were lodging operators, who collect the tax, consulted?
  • If resiliency grants are used, who qualifies as a small business and how are awards measured?
  • How will event grant recipients be asked to show visitor and room night results?
