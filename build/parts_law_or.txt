<h2>What Oregon lodging tax dollars can legally fund</h2>
<p class="lede2">Oregon cities and counties levy their own transient lodging taxes (84 cities and 15 counties as of 2025) under ORS 320.300 to 320.365. The state does not run a competitive grant program for local dollars and, until 2027, has never required local governments to report how they spend them. The rules that matter are a short list of definitions in ORS 320.300 and a spending split in ORS 320.350 that applies only to taxes imposed or raised after July 1, 2003. The 2026 Legislature rewrote that split: the tourism share drops from 70 percent to 50 percent on January 1, 2027, and a biennial spending report to the Legislature begins the same year.</p>
<div class="cols">
<div>
<h3>Permitted uses, ORS 320.350</h3>
<ul>
<li>Net revenue from a new or increased local tax may only be used, under ORS 320.350(5), to "(a) Fund tourism promotion, tourism-related facilities" (from January 1, 2027, also "or resiliency grants for small businesses in the restaurant and lodging industry"); "(b) Fund city or county services; or (c) Finance or refinance the debt of tourism-related facilities and pay reasonable administrative costs."</li>
<li>"Tourism promotion" is defined in ORS 320.300 as "any of the following activities: (a) Advertising, publicizing or distributing information for the purpose of attracting and welcoming tourists; (b) Conducting strategic planning and research necessary to stimulate future tourism development; (c) Operating tourism promotion agencies; and (d) Marketing special events and festivals designed to attract tourists."</li>
<li>"Tourism-related facility" means "(a) A conference center, convention center or visitor information center; and (b) Other improved real property that has a useful life of 10 or more years and has a substantial purpose of supporting tourism or accommodating tourist activities." A temporary event is not a facility; a permanent stage, restroom block or event field can be.</li>
<li>"Tourist" means "a person who, for business, pleasure, recreation or participation in events related to the arts, heritage or culture, travels from the community in which that person is a resident to a different community that is separate, distinct from and unrelated to the person's community of residence, and that trip: (a) Requires the person to travel more than 50 miles from the community of residence; or (b) Includes an overnight stay." "Tourism" means "economic activity resulting from tourists."</li>
<li>Oregon Restaurant and Lodging Assn. v. City of Bend, 313 Or App 772 (Aug. 11, 2021): Bend cut its tourism share from 35.4 to 31.2 percent and the Court of Appeals held that violated the plain text of ORS 320.350(3). Until 2027 the share a city spends on tourism cannot go down.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div>
<h3>Who can apply</h3>
<ul>
<li>The statute does not say who may receive the money. It restricts purposes, not recipients, and nothing in ORS 320.300 to 320.365 limits grants or contracts to nonprofits. The "tourism promotion agency" definition names nonprofits and governmental units, but that defines the agency a city may fund to run its destination marketing, not the universe of eligible payees.</li>
<li>Eligibility is set locally and varies. Salem's TOT grants go mostly to 501(c)(3), (c)(4) or (c)(6) organizations, with grants of $2,000 or less open to groups without 501(c) status. Philomath's TLT grant accepts any "verifiable non-profit service organization or business." The League of Oregon Cities tells members that pass-throughs to "chambers of commerce or event organizers" are common and that cities "should carefully restrict use of such revenues" and require reporting.</li>
<li>No advisory committee is required by state law. The new HB 4148 report asks only for "a summary of advisory board meetings or oversight actions, if applicable." Many cities have one anyway (Salem's Cultural and Tourism Promotion Advisory Board, Newberg's TLT committee), and many others contract the restricted share to a destination management organization such as Visit Bend or Travel Lane County, which then runs its own grant or sponsorship process.</li>
<li>Statewide, Travel Oregon's Competitive Grants ($20,000 to $150,000, funded from the state tax) are open only to local governments, port districts, federally recognized tribes and Oregon-registered nonprofits. A for-profit producer needs a public or nonprofit applicant for that program.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div>
<h3>The 70 / 30 split</h3>
<ul>
<li>Current text, ORS 320.350(6): "At least 70 percent of net revenue from a new or increased local transient lodging tax shall be used for the purposes described in subsection (5)(a) or (c) of this section. No more than 30 percent of net revenue from a new or increased local transient lodging tax may be used for the purpose described in subsection (5)(b) of this section."</li>
<li>From January 1, 2027 (HB 4148, Oregon Laws 2026, chapter 121): "At least 50 percent of net revenue from a new or increased local transient lodging tax shall be used for the purposes described in subsection (5)(a) or (c)" and "No more than 50 percent ... may be used for the purpose described in subsection (5)(b)." The restricted half also gains the new "resiliency grants" purpose.</li>
<li>Grandfathering: the split applies only to a tax, or the portion of a rate, imposed or increased after July 1, 2003. Revenue from a pre-2003 rate is unrestricted except that ORS 320.350(3) says a city "may not decrease the percentage of total local transient lodging tax revenues that are actually expended to fund tourism promotion or tourism-related facilities on or after July 2, 2003." That freeze is why Bend lost in court, and why the tourism share differs so much from city to city.</li>
<li>HB 4148 unlocks the freeze. New subsection (7)(c) lets a local government, "in accordance with subsection (6) of this section, decrease the percentage of total local transient lodging tax revenues that are actually expended to fund tourism promotion or tourism-related facilities regardless of when the percentage went into effect," and (7)(a) and (b) let unexpended pre-2027 revenue be reallocated the same way. Expect councils to revisit tourism budgets in 2027.</li>
<li>Reporting: until 2027 there is no state reporting requirement at all, no Oregon equivalent of Washington's JLARC form. Section 4 of HB 4148 requires each taxing city and county to file a report with the Legislative Revenue Officer by September 1 of each odd-numbered year starting 2027, showing rates split by pre- and post-2003 status, total revenue, and "the amount and percentage of revenue used to fund tourism promotion, including revenue expended under agreements with destination management organizations," tourism-related facilities, resiliency grants and city or county services. DMOs and other recipients must report to the city for inclusion. The League of Oregon Cities or the Association of Oregon Counties may file on members' behalf.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div>
<h3>How decisions are made</h3>
<ul>
<li>Locally, by ordinance and budget. Each city or county sets its own rate, its own grant program and its own eligibility rules, so the process in Bend (contract to Visit Bend, with a city tourism fund) looks nothing like Salem (annual TOT grant round scored by an advisory board, council decides) or Philomath (small annual RFP from the finance department). Most jurisdictions award once a year during the spring budget cycle.</li>
<li>Two layers above the city. The state tax (1.5 percent, administered by the Oregon Department of Revenue) funds the Oregon Tourism Commission, which does business as Travel Oregon. ORS 284.131 directs at least 65 percent to state tourism programs, 10 percent to a competitive grant program for "tourism-related facilities and tourism-generating events, including sporting events," and 20 percent to the Regional Cooperative Tourism Program, divided among seven regional destination management organizations in proportion to the lodging tax collected in each region. RDMOs such as Visit Central Oregon and Travel Oregon's own grant rounds are the only competitive statewide pots, and they fund public and nonprofit applicants.</li>
<li>The state rate rises to 2.75 percent on January 1, 2027 under HB 4134 (Oregon Laws 2026, chapter 140). The 1.25 point increase must appear on receipts as a "nature conservation fee" and goes to the Recovering Oregon's Wildlife Fund, not to tourism.</li>
<li>Assessment districts exist alongside the tax. Portland's Tourism Improvement District (2 percent on guest room revenue at hotels over 50 rooms, since October 2012) is run by Travel Portland for sales and marketing and is governed by city ordinance, not ORS 320. Hotel-funded TIDs answer to their hotel boards and can sponsor room-night-generating events on business terms that a tax-funded grant cannot.</li>
<li>Recent history of the rules: HB 2494 (2023, a voter-approved 3 percent local tax for law enforcement and emergency services) died in House Rules. HB 3556 and HB 3962 (2025, adding public safety and infrastructure as permitted uses and shifting the split as far as 60/40 unrestricted) died; HB 3962 stalled in a Senate committee. HB 4148 (2026, the "LOCAL Act," chief sponsors Rep. Cyrus Javadi, Rep. Jules Walters, Sen. Courtney Neron Misslin, Sen. Suzanne Weber) passed the House 40 to 12 on February 25 and the Senate 23 to 6 on March 5, 2026 after the Oregon Restaurant and Lodging Association moved from opposition to neutral once the floor was set at 50 percent. The League of Oregon Cities called it a long-term priority.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<details>
<summary>How to raise it with a council or tourism committee</summary>
<div class="talk">
<p>Lead with the definition, not the event. Under ORS 320.300 a request to fund "marketing special events and festivals designed to attract tourists" is squarely inside "tourism promotion," so ask the city to fund the marketing and promotion of the event, priced as a line item, rather than asking it to underwrite production. Show that the audience meets the statutory "tourist" test: people who travel more than 50 miles or stay overnight. Bring a room-night estimate, a list of hotels you will block, and a plan to measure it, because starting in 2027 every Oregon city has to report the amount and percentage of lodging tax it spent on tourism promotion and will want numbers it can defend.</p>
<p>Ask which bucket you are in. Every city has some mix of pre-2003 revenue (no restriction except the frozen percentage), post-2003 restricted revenue (70 percent tourism now, 50 percent from January 2027) and a contract with a destination management organization that already holds most of the restricted share. If the DMO holds the money, your real audience is its board or event grant program, and the city can only point you there. If the city holds a tourism fund, ask the finance director what the fund balance is and when the annual grant round opens; many cities carry unspent restricted revenue because they cannot find qualifying projects, and HB 4148's new reporting line for "revenue held in capital reserves" will make that visible.</p>
<p>Do not propose that lodging tax pay for police overtime, road closures or housing as a tourism expense. Those are "city or county services" under ORS 320.350(5)(b), paid only from the unrestricted share, and after the Bend decision and the 2026 debate councils know the line. A multi-year event with a permanent installation (a stage, restrooms, a field) can be framed as a "tourism-related facility" if it is improved real property with a useful life of 10 or more years and the city owns it. Being a for-profit producer is not a bar under state law; it is a bar in some local grant rules (Salem) and in Travel Oregon's statewide grants, so for those bring a nonprofit or municipal co-applicant and let the producer be the contractor.</p>
